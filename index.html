Case study 01 · Sep 2026 – Present

Multi-Factor Berth Scheduling Simulator

Supply Chain Digitalisation & Design (40.318) industry project with Cargill Ocean Transportation

PythonSimPyDiscrete-event simulationScenario analysispytestPort operations
432vessel calls over a one-year horizon
3scheduling rules compared on identical mechanics
4scenarios, from baseline to stress conditions

The problem

Port scheduling rules are usually judged under "average" conditions. A rule that looks fine on average can break down when bad weather, a surge of arrivals, or both hit at once. These disruptions are rare and tangled together in historical data, so they are hard to study from history alone.

Our brief was to build a simulated port, deliberately push disruptions through it, and see which rules hold up. The output is a stress-testing method, not a single "best" policy.

The approach

  • Built a discrete-event simulation of the terminal in SimPy using Cargill's data pack (arrivals, berth capabilities, service profiles, weather).
  • Validated the baseline against the data pack's capacity check (load ratio 0.73).
  • Compared three rules: FCFS, Earliest Deadline First and Size priority, on three metrics: average wait, 95th-percentile wait and throughput.
  • Kept berth choice, weather and outages identical across simulators, so any difference in results comes from the rule alone.

My role

  • Built the size-priority simulator and its test suite, which slots into the team's shared runner without changes to the existing code.
  • Verified all three heuristics compute the metrics identically by recalculating every result independently from per-ship logs.
  • Analysed and presented the S1 baseline results (3 rules × 3 metrics) in the team's progress presentation.

Key findings so far

  • Under baseline conditions the rules are nearly indistinguishable: average wait spans 6.63–7.10 h and median wait is 0 h for all three.
  • The differences show up in the tail. Size priority has the longest 95th-percentile wait (30.8 h), as smaller vessels can be passed over repeatedly by larger ones.
  • This is why stress scenarios matter: a rule's weaknesses only appear when the port is under pressure.

Engineering practices

  • Shared runner auto-discovers every *_simulator.py, so new rules plug in without editing existing files.
  • Tests check rule ordering, reordering behaviour and exact continuity with FCFS when the priority key is swapped.
  • Deliberately planted bugs to confirm the tests would catch them.

Project ongoing. Stress-scenario results (S2–S4) to be added.

Risk Prediction Model for Escalation of Care in CGH@Home Patients

Design & Business Analytics Industry Partnership with Changi General Hospital · Data Analyst

The problem

CGH@Home delivers hospital-level care in patients' own homes under the national MIC@Home programme. Some patients deteriorate and must "U-turn" back to the hospital. Our question was:

How might we build an accurate risk prediction model that estimates each CGH@Home patient's likelihood of readmission and identifies the key risk factors?

The approach

  • Cleaning: handled NULL/UNKNOWN values in Excel; Power BI used for visual data-quality checks.
  • Feature selection: Random Forest importance scores (> 0.10 threshold).
  • Model: CatBoost gradient boosting, which handles high-cardinality categorical features well and copes with small datasets.
  • Imbalance: the first model predicted no U-turns at all, so we used SMOTE to generate synthetic minority samples.

My role

  • Developed and evaluated classification models in Python (logistic regression, decision trees) to identify key risk factors.
  • Designed interactive Power BI dashboards that let clinicians explore risk factors and prediction outcomes.

Takeaways and limitations

High accuracy on an imbalanced dataset can hide a useless model, so recall on the minority class mattered more than the headline number. The model is limited by 1.5 years of data and its reliance on synthetic samples. Future work would validate it on fresh real-world admissions before clinical use.

Case study 03 · Jan – May 2024

ARDUNE: Interactive Sand Art Installation

Design Thinking & Innovation Project, SUTD · Project Coordinator, Data & Code

PythonArduinoElectronicsCADData analysisTop 3 in class

The idea

Waiting for the train is dead time. ARDUNE is an interactive sand-art table for commuters: users steer a steel ball across a bed of sand with a joystick. A motorised magnet under the surface drags the ball, drawing patterns that can be wiped and redrawn endlessly. Hint buttons and LED lighting guide users and make the piece eye-catching on a platform.

The team's prototype was ranked among the top 3 innovations in the class.

My role

  • Code & electronics: wrote Python code to get the Arduino, motors and electronics working together.
  • Data analysis: analysed the results from our user testing to inform design iterations.
  • Project coordination: made sure everyone knew their role and kept the team on track for deadlines and presentations.
